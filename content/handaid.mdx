## Overview

HandAid is a 4-degree-of-freedom robot arm built to help hospital patients with limited mobility feed themselves. For someone recovering from surgery, living with a spinal cord injury, or dealing with tremors, eating usually means waiting for a nurse or family member. HandAid aims to give some of that independence back with a small bedside arm that brings food from the plate to the patient's mouth.

The arm is a serial chain of four revolute joints, driven by daisy-chained Dynamixel servos mounted on 3D-printed links. I drafted the initial mechanical design in SOLIDWORKS, wrote a Julia package that solves the arm's forward and inverse kinematics from its Denavit–Hartenberg (DH) parameters, built a MATLAB app to simulate and visualize its paths, and wrote the servo position control with the Dynamixel SDK, including PS4 controller input for teleoperation.

## Background

Feeding assistance is one of the most common and most time-consuming tasks in inpatient care, and it's one where losing independence hits hard. Commercial feeding robots exist, but they're expensive, bulky, and often limited to fixed scoop-and-lift motions that don't adapt to different plates, foods, or patient positions.

Being next to a hospital bed puts real constraints on the design:

- **Safety:** the arm works near a patient's face, so its motion has to be slow, predictable, and low-force.
- **Footprint:** it has to fit on an overbed table without getting in the way of care.
- **Cost and serviceability:** printed parts and off-the-shelf servos keep it cheap and easy to repair.
- **Simple control:** the patient, or a caregiver, needs to be able to operate it with minimal training.

## Design

The arm uses four revolute joints. A **base** joint swings the arm between the plate and the patient. **Shoulder and elbow** joints set reach and height. A **wrist** joint at the end of the forearm turns the 3D-printed utensil holder to tilt the spoon.

![HandAid CAD model](/handaid-assets/handaid-model.png)

The links are 3D-printed, which made it fast to iterate on lengths and joint housings. Each joint is driven by a Dynamixel servo, mostly MX-28AR units. The servos are daisy-chained on a single bus, so the whole arm runs on one cable, and each servo provides closed-loop position control and reports its own position.

## Kinematics

The arm is described with standard DH parameters, one row per joint. From those, the Julia package builds each joint's homogeneous transform and multiplies them along the chain:

- **Forward kinematics:** given the four joint angles, compute the pose of the spoon.
- **Inverse kinematics:** given a target spoon position (the plate, or the patient's mouth), solve for the joint angles that reach it.

Before running anything on hardware, every path was checked in a MATLAB app that animates the arm along a planned trajectory and plots its moving coordinate frames. That catches unreachable targets, joint-limit violations, and awkward elbow flips on screen instead of on the robot.

## Conclusion

HandAid brought my mechanical, math, and software work together in one project. The kinematics are textbook. Most of the effort went into the details around them: keeping simulation and hardware consistent, picking DH frames that keep the math manageable, and designing printed parts that hold up under servo loads.

Future directions:

- **Closed-loop targeting:** add a camera so the arm finds the plate and the patient's mouth itself, instead of using preset positions.
- **Compliance and safety:** use servo current sensing to detect contact and stop, important for any device near a person's face.
- **Accessible input:** add voice, sip-and-puff, or head-tracking controls for patients who can't use a gamepad.
